# Mutual fund knowledge base
Written in simple language for beginners. Each "##" section is one searchable topic for the AI assistant.
Tax figures were checked in October 2026; tax rules change with budgets, so always confirm the latest rules.

## What is a mutual fund?
A mutual fund pools money from many investors and a professional fund manager invests it in shares, bonds or both. You get "units" in the fund. If the investments grow, the value of your units grows. It lets small investors own a diversified portfolio (many companies at once) starting from as little as ₹100-₹500.

## What is SIP?
SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) means investing a fixed amount every month (for example ₹2,000 on the 5th) automatically from your bank account. Benefits: builds a saving habit, you don't need a large amount at once, and you automatically buy more units when prices are low and fewer when prices are high (called rupee cost averaging). SIPs can be paused, increased or stopped any time.

## SIP vs lumpsum: which is better?
Lumpsum means investing a big amount at once. If markets rise steadily, lumpsum can earn more because all money is invested early. But SIP reduces the risk of investing everything just before a market fall. For salaried people, SIP is the natural choice. If you have a big amount, you can invest it gradually through an STP (Systematic Transfer Plan) from a liquid fund into an equity fund over 6-12 months.

## What is NAV?
NAV (Net Asset Value) is the price of one unit of a mutual fund, declared every business day. A low NAV does NOT mean a fund is cheap or better; what matters is how fast the NAV grows (returns), not its absolute value.

## What is an expense ratio?
The expense ratio is the yearly fee the fund charges, as a % of your investment. It is deducted from the fund automatically, so returns you see are already after this fee. A difference of 1% per year can reduce your final amount by 15-20% over 20 years, so lower is better. Index funds and Direct plans usually have the lowest expense ratios.

## Direct plan vs Regular plan
Every fund has two versions. Regular plans pay commission to a distributor/agent, so their expense ratio is higher. Direct plans have no commission, so they have a lower expense ratio and give higher returns on the same portfolio. If you invest on your own through the AMC website or apps that offer direct plans, choose the Direct plan.

## Types of mutual funds: equity, debt, hybrid
Equity funds invest mainly in company shares: highest long-term growth, but can fall 20-40% in bad years; suitable for goals 5+ years away. Debt funds invest in bonds, government securities and money-market instruments: lower risk, steadier returns, similar to fixed deposits; suitable for goals under 3 years. Hybrid funds mix both, giving a middle path.

## Large cap, mid cap and small cap funds
Large cap funds invest in the 100 biggest companies (stable, lower risk). Mid cap funds invest in companies ranked 101-250 (more growth, more ups and downs). Small cap funds invest in smaller companies (highest growth potential and highest risk; can fall 50%+ in a crash). Flexi cap and multi cap funds invest across all sizes. Beginners usually start with large cap, flexi cap or index funds.

## What is an index fund?
An index fund simply copies a market index like NIFTY 50 instead of a manager picking stocks. Because it is passive, the expense ratio is very low (often 0.1-0.3%). Many active funds fail to beat the index over long periods, so index funds are a simple, low-cost choice for beginners.

## What is ELSS and how does it save tax?
ELSS (Equity Linked Savings Scheme) is an equity fund with a 3-year lock-in. Under the OLD tax regime, investments up to ₹1.5 lakh per year in ELSS can be deducted from taxable income under Section 80C. This deduction is NOT available under the new tax regime. ELSS has the shortest lock-in among 80C options (PPF has 15 years).

## Tax on mutual funds: tax rates on gains (STCG and LTCG tax)
Equity funds: if you sell within 12 months, gains are short-term and taxed at 20%. If held more than 12 months, long-term gains above ₹1.25 lakh in a financial year are taxed at 12.5% (the first ₹1.25 lakh is tax-free). Debt funds bought on or after 1 April 2023: gains are taxed at your income-tax slab rate, whatever the holding period. Dividends (IDCW) are added to your income and taxed at your slab rate. Rules change with budgets, so check the latest before selling.

## What is a riskometer / risk level?
Every Indian mutual fund must show a SEBI riskometer with 6 levels: Low, Low to Moderate, Moderate, Moderately High, High, Very High. Most equity funds are Very High. This app's dataset has these levels as 1 to 6, and the advisor only recommends funds up to the risk level that suits your profile.

## What is exit load?
An exit load is a small fee (often 1%) charged if you withdraw within a short time, usually within 1 year for equity funds. Liquid funds have a tiny exit load for the first few days. Check the exit load before investing money you may need soon.

## How much return can I expect?
Historically in India, diversified equity funds have given roughly 10-14% per year over long periods (10+ years), debt funds about 6-8%, and hybrid funds in between. Returns are never guaranteed and can be negative in some years. This app uses about 12% for equity, 9.5% for hybrid and 7% for debt in the goal planner.

## What is compounding?
Compounding means your returns also earn returns. ₹1 lakh at 12% becomes about ₹3.1 lakh in 10 years, ₹9.6 lakh in 20 years and ₹30 lakh in 30 years. The longer you stay invested, the faster money grows, which is why starting early matters more than investing a big amount later.

## Step-up SIP (top-up SIP): increasing your SIP every year
A step-up (top-up) SIP increases your monthly SIP by a fixed % every year, for example 10% when your salary increases. Even a small yearly increase makes a big difference: ₹5,000/month at 12% for 20 years gives about ₹46 lakh, but with a 10% yearly step-up it gives about ₹93 lakh, roughly double.

## What is SWP (Systematic Withdrawal Plan)?
SWP lets you withdraw a fixed amount every month from your mutual fund investment, like a pension. The remaining money keeps growing. It is commonly used after retirement. If your withdrawal rate is low (for example 4-6% of the corpus per year), the money can last for decades.

## How do I start investing in mutual funds?
1) Complete KYC once (PAN, Aadhaar, bank account; done online in minutes). 2) Choose a platform: the fund house (AMC) website, or an app/platform that offers Direct plans. 3) Pick funds that match your goal and risk. 4) Start a SIP with auto-debit. 5) Review once a year.

## Emergency fund before investing
Before investing in equity funds, keep 3-6 months of expenses in a savings account, FD or liquid fund as an emergency fund. Otherwise you may be forced to sell investments at a loss during an emergency. Also have health insurance and, if anyone depends on you, term life insurance.

## Should I stop SIP when the market falls?
No. When markets fall, your SIP buys more units at lower prices, which increases returns when markets recover. Stopping SIPs in a fall locks in fear and misses the recovery. Only stop if your goal has changed or you genuinely need the money.

## How many mutual funds should I own?
Usually 3-5 funds are enough, for example one large cap or index fund, one flexi/mid cap fund, one debt or hybrid fund, and ELSS if you need tax saving. Owning too many funds creates overlap (same companies) and makes tracking harder without adding much diversification.

## What are CAGR, XIRR and absolute return?
Absolute return is total % growth (₹100 to ₹150 = 50%). CAGR is the average yearly growth rate for a one-time investment. XIRR is the correct yearly return for SIPs, because each instalment is invested at a different time. When comparing funds, look at 3-year and 5-year CAGR, not just last year.

## What are Sharpe ratio, standard deviation and beta?
Standard deviation measures how much a fund's returns jump around (volatility); higher means riskier. Sharpe ratio measures return per unit of risk; higher is better. Beta compares a fund's movement with the market: beta 1.2 means it moves about 20% more than the market. This app uses Sharpe and Sortino ratios when scoring funds.

## How does this app pick funds?
The app first finds your risk profile from 5 questions (age, time horizon, income stability, reaction to a fall, experience). Then it splits your money into equity, hybrid and debt. Within each group it ranks funds by a score: 35% machine-learning predicted 3-year return, 25% Sharpe ratio, 15% Sortino ratio, 15% low expense ratio and 10% rating, and only allows funds whose risk level suits you.

## How does the market model work?
A machine learning model (Random Forest) looks at recent NIFTY 50 behaviour (momentum, volatility, moving averages, RSI) and estimates the chance the market will be higher in one month. Its accuracy is only slightly better than guessing, which is normal because markets are hard to predict, so the app only shifts your allocation by at most 10% based on it.

## Is this financial advice?
No. This is an academic project built on historical data. Past returns do not guarantee future returns. Before investing real money, consult a SEBI-registered investment advisor and read the scheme documents carefully.
